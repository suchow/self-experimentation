In 1951, Jay Traver, a zoologist at the University of Massachusetts and an authority on New World mayflies, published a paper in the Proceedings of the Entomological Society of Washington describing what she believed was an infestation of her own scalp. She reported small itching red papules as early as 1934. In 1943, she wrote, sensations of crawling, scratching, and biting became pronounced, worst between about ten at night and the early morning. She reported that mites invaded her eyes, ears, and nostrils and affected two other women in her family. In her report, she described trials of DDT powder, kerosene emulsion, a mange cure for cats, Lysol, sulphur ointment, and many other substances. She reported that none eliminated the mites and that one made them migrate. According to Traver, a dermatologist judged her symptoms largely imaginary and, after three days in hospital, referred her to a neurologist for her "psychoneurotic" condition. She wrote that she convinced the neurologist she had no need of his services. She collected specimens that Baker, a government specialist, identified as Dermatophagoides scheremetewskyi. Fain (1967) later reassessed two Traver specimens supplied by Baker: a female D. pteronyssinus and a male D. farinae. Hinkle (2000) interpreted her published account as a classic case of delusory parasitosis, the fixed false belief that one's body is infested. Shelomi (2013) likewise classified the account as delusory parasitosis and discussed possible retraction.
